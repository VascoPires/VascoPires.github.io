In [Part 2: Taking Advantage of Symmetries](../abaqus_tutorial_part2/) we reduced the plate to a quarter and used the saved elements to refine the mesh. However, the mesh was still *free*: Abaqus decided where the elements go, and it put as many elements far away from the hole (where nothing happens) as close to it (where everything happens).

There is a second reason to be careful with the mesh. The **Abaqus Learning Edition** (the one you can install at home) is limited to **1000 nodes**. So we cannot just keep reducing the seed size until the results look nice. If our goal is to analyze what is happening around the hole, then we need to add more elements there. That is the goal of this tutorial.

The usual way to do this is to **partition** the part into simple regions, which can then be meshed with a **structured** mesh that follows the shape of the hole.

The rest of the model (material, section, step, symmetry conditions and the RP with the kinematic coupling) is exactly the same as in [Part 2: Taking Advantage of Symmetries](../abaqus_tutorial_part2/).

## ✂️ Partitioning 

The idea is shown in [Figure 1](#fig-t3-partitions) (a). We split the quarter plate into three regions with two lines:

1. A horizontal line at $$y = 10$$ mm, which closes a $$10 \times 10$$ mm square around the hole.
2. A diagonal line at 45°, from the hole edge at $$(3.54, 3.54)$$ to the corner of the square at $$(10, 10)$$.

Regions 1 and 2 now have four sides each and region 3 is a simple rectangle. Partitioning the model is nothing more than "organizing" our geometry in the way we want it. It is often useful to define different material properties, different sections or different meshes. In this case we are using it to improve the mesh quality so that we can use a [structured mesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_mesh#Structured_grids). And, if you are wondering how to choose these partitions, there is no single right answer. It is mostly a matter of common practices. Around holes, the usual approach is a 45° line from the hole to the corner of a square “box” around it. The 45° line splits the region around the hole into four-sided pieces, which is what a structured mesh needs. The box should be large enough that the stress has almost returned to its far-field value at its edge. Here we placed it at 10 mm, twice the hole radius, but this distance can be higher or shorter. Any partition that gives four-sided regions and puts small elements near the hole works just as well.

In the **Part** module, there are several partitioning tools and it may seem overwhelming initially. All we need for this example are the 2D partitioning tools, which are these icons <img class="tut-icon" src="../../assets/img/tutorials/abaqus_plate_with_hole/icons-partitions.png" alt=""> . The one on the left partitions an edge and the one on the right partitions a face (or an area). 

The video below shows the two steps:

1. **Partition Edge**: split the hole arc at its midpoint. This creates the vertex at $$(3.54, 3.54)$$ where the diagonal will start.
2. **Partition Face**: in the sketch, draw the horizontal line at $$y = 10$$ mm and the diagonal from the new vertex to the corner $$(10, 10)$$, then click *Done*.

## 🧱 Mesh controls

Now, in the **Mesh** module, we tell Abaqus how to mesh each region. Change the mesh controls (second row & second column in the mesh module <img class="tut-icon" src="../../assets/img/tutorials/abaqus_plate_with_hole/icon-meshcontrols.png" alt="">) to: 
$$ \text{Quad} \; \rightarrow  \text{Structured}$$
Select all three regions at once. This will change the colour of your regions from pink (free) to green (structured). If a region turns orange, Abaqus cannot mesh it with this technique, which usually means the partition lines do not meet the edges exactly.

## 🌱 Seeding the edges

Instead of a global seed size, we now seed the edges one by one with a fixed **number of elements**. Go to the seed icon <img class="tut-icon" src="../../assets/img/tutorials/abaqus_plate_with_hole/icon-seededge.png" alt=""> or:



$$\text{Seed} \; \rightarrow \; \text{Edges} \; \rightarrow \; \text{Method: By number}$$



For the edges going away from the hole we also add a **bias**, which makes the elements small near the hole and larger further away. Choose $$\text{Bias: Single}$$ and set the bias ratio (largest element / smallest element). After applying the seeds, check in the viewport that the seed marks are closer together near the hole; if they are the wrong way round, use **Flip** and pick the edge again.

## Did it help?

[Figure 5](#fig-t3-mesh-zoom) compares the free mesh from Part 2 with the new partitioned mesh, near the hole. They have almost the same number of nodes, but in the partitioned mesh the elements follow the hole and are much smaller exactly where the stress is highest.

Table 2 shows the highest stress in the loading direction, $$\sigma_{22}$$, at the edge of the hole. To know which value is "right", I also ran a reference model with a very fine mesh (far too many for the Learning Edition), whose peak no longer changes when the mesh is refined.

| ----------------------- | ----- | ------------------------- | ----- |
| Free, seed 0.8 (Part 2) | 955   | 274                       | −19 % |

With the same number of nodes, the partitioned mesh reduces the error from 19 % to 4 %.  This peak is exactly what we are interested in in Part 5: Stress Concentrations.

## 🔢 Linear vs quadratic elements

There is one more choice we have not touched yet. The **element type**. So far we have been using the Abaqus default for a 2D planar part, **CPS4R**: **C**ontinuum, **P**lane **S**tress, **4** nodes, reduced integration. This is a **linear** element: the displacement varies linearly between its 4 corner nodes, so the stress is (almost) constant inside each element. To follow a stress that changes quickly, like at the hole, we need many small elements, and each one only gives us the *average* stress over its area, which is always a bit lower than the true peak.

A **quadratic** element, **CPS8R**, has an extra node in the middle of every edge (8 nodes in total, [Figure 6](#fig-t3-elements) (a)). The displacement now varies quadratically, so the stress can vary inside the element. The mathematics behind it  is not important at the moment, but if you are curious, Klaus-Jürgen Bathe explains how these elements are built in his free MIT lecture [Formulation and Calculation of Isoparametric Models](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-2-002-finite-element-procedures-for-solids-and-structures-spring-2010/resources/lecture-6/).

Of course, we pay a price for using quadratic elements, we have more nodes per element: with the same 1000-node budget we get fewer, larger elements as shown in [Figure 6](#fig-t3-elements).

Mesh near the hole with (b) linear elements and (c) quadratic elements, with all their nodes shown in red. Both meshes have fewer than 1000 nodes."><figcaption class="caption">(a) Linear (4-node) and quadratic (8-node) elements. Mesh near the hole with (b) linear elements and (c) quadratic elements, with all their nodes shown in red. Both meshes have fewer than 1000 nodes.</figcaption></figure>

Now, inside Abaqus, to switch the element type, in the **Mesh** module go to element type <img class="tut-icon" src="../../assets/img/tutorials/abaqus_plate_with_hole/icon-element-type.png" alt=""> : 



$$\text{Mesh} \; \rightarrow \; \text{Element Type} \; \rightarrow \; \text{select all regions} \; \rightarrow \; \text{Geometric Order: Quadratic}$$



Keep *Family: Plane Stress* and *Reduced integration* ticked; the description at the bottom of the dialog should say **CPS8R**. Because every element now carries its midside nodes, the 12/20/30 seeds would give 2669 nodes, so we need to reduce the mesh to **7 per arc, 12 radial and 18 vertical** (same biases as before, and 7 on the square sides and the top edge) to fit the student version. If you mesh it again and you Query it, you should have **971 nodes and 294 elements**. Obviously, if you are not limited by the Student Version, you can keep the previous seeding.

<figure class="article-figure" id="fig-meh-type-ps"><img class="img-fluid rounded tut-landscape" src="../../assets/img/tutorials/abaqus_plate_with_hole/meh-type-ps.png" alt="Element Type dialog with Geometric Order: Quadratic, Family: Plane Stress and Reduced integration; the description at the bottom reads CPS8R."><figcaption class="caption">Element Type dialog with Geometric Order: Quadratic, Family: Plane Stress and Reduced integration; the description at the bottom reads CPS8R.</figcaption></figure>

How much better is it? [Figure 8](#fig-t3-convergence) shows a small convergence study. The same partitioned model meshed coarser and finer, with both element types.

The grey area is beyond the 1000-node limit of the Learning Edition."><figcaption class="caption">Peak stress at the hole against the number of nodes, for linear and quadratic elements. As seen in the results the difference between the amout of nodes we need for reach convergence for quadratic and linear is huge. The quadratic mesh is within **0.5 %** of the converged value with fewer than 1000 nodes (and about 1 % off already with 464 nodes). The linear elements approach the answer from below, and they do get there close to the solution until we used around 200 000 nodes.  

<div class="callout tip" markdown="1">
<div class="callout-title"><span class="callout-icon">💡</span><span>Tip</span></div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

As a rule of thumb, use **quadratic** elements when you care about stresses in a smooth part, like here. Linear elements are fine for displacements and stiffness, and are the usual choice in explicit dynamics and some contact problems, where you need many elements anyway.
